How normal is the normalcy that we are taught?

“This is how things are normally supposed to be.” And the normalcy being talked about here is usually just family dynamics infested with internalised misogyny, blatant patriarchy and casual sexism, along with a terrible case of casteism and classism, wrapped in a floral packaging we like to call ‘Values’. Anyone questioning these so-called values is labelled ‘tez/muhfatt’(outspoken) and is isolated and outcasted, or leaves voluntarily due to being treated with hostility from the same people that were once family.

Upon stepping out, the definition of ‘Normal’ changes constantly based on the kind of environment we choose to live and work in, and the people we surround ourselves with. We get to experiment being in different spaces, we’re free to find spaces we resonate with and feel safe in. We get to be curious without being shut down in the name of ‘sanskar’. We get to question our identity and express ourselves in a way that feels the most authentic to us. We get to build a life and personality all on our own once we’re free from the shackles of ‘normal’ we grew up bound with.

The day I freed myself from these shackles and had my idea of ‘normal’ completely shaken, I realised I didn’t have to disfigure my personality to fit into a broken, poorly taped together normalcy. I still have a lot of learning and unlearning to do. While I do that, I let my heart run wild and always hope for better because I see myself trying, and winning because I see growth.

A recent event that led me to this train of thought was when I hosted an intimate dinner for my friends one last time at the place I was going to move out of. My mother had also come over so I used that opportunity to introduce my friends to her and brag about my prospering social life. Perfect chance to show them off and maybe evolve my mother’s perspective and mindset, even by a single thought. I wanted her to see how different my normal was from hers and why I couldn’t stand staying back in my hometown.

That day, around afternoon, they started arriving one by one. And like second nature to them, they settled into the space with a lovely sense of belongingness, moving around with practiced ease and putting away things that they had brought, getting whatever they needed all by themselves, settling down wherever they pleased, so in tune with each other and the space like they were family. They are family. The entire evening felt like a scene from a coming of age indie film, although we were all in our twenties.

Everybody was involved in cooking one way or the other, there were multiple rounds of coffee and snacking. Singing breaks in the middle of dinner preparation, conversations swinging like a child with sugar rush from career discussion to poorly delivered jokes. Then everyone picked a task they felt gravitated towards, rather than following the “normal” segregation of chores.

Although more often than I would be happy to admit, we found ourselves (especially the women) doing things that we were taught as our ‘duties’. These were habits instilled in us by watching generations of women before us. They were not grand gestures but things that often go unnoticed in a family normally, something that we, the ones challenging the norms, also considered normal but were very much charged by patriarchy. Things like always taking the burnt roti, or making sure everyone’s plates were full and there was enough food for seconds even at the cost of not having one full portion to ourselves, serving everyone ourselves and cleaning up after everyone. Luckily, with me and my friends, these actions were generally accompanied or followed by acknowledgement and unlearning. The people I chose to be around played a big role in the process of unlearning these behaviors along with choosing to do things differently. The burnt roti would be gone in seconds, everyone’s plates were full, everyone served themselves and each other, everyone played their part in cleaning up. Something I consider normal but was far away from my mother’s normal, who was probably endeared as well but chose not to show it.

It felt very euphoric, a night filled with realisations of how all the effort I had put in to build a life like this was worth it. I was not forcing myself to have superficial conversations that I did not want to be a part of, how it was a beautiful balance of fun and sincerity, love and boundaries. A world away from all the viciousness we learned growing up. A world where I felt safe, where I did not have to watch my mouth or read somebody’s mind. A world where I did not have to scramble to justify simple misunderstood actions or beg for explanations. Here, communication felt like breathing and conversations flowed like water, smiles were cherished and tears were respected. This was a world with space for all versions of you, unapologetic you.

I could go on and on about that day and all the others that had given me a sense of normalcy in my own skin. This was the world of my dreams that I was finally living in. It was not some magical fairyland away from the supposed “reality” of society. It was on the same planet, same land, same neighborhood, shared the same air as those who had different ‘normal’s, the normal “unwritten rules”, “way of the world”, “traditions” (that are usually meant to clip your wings). My world lived right adjacent to worlds like these, my normal lived right next to the other normals.
